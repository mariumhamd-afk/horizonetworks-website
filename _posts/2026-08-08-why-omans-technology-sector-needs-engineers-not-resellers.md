# Why Oman's Technology Sector Needs Engineers, Not Resellers

*Published by Horizon Networks | Talk to our engineering team*

Oman's ICT market has never looked more promising. The Sultanate's technology spending reached [$5.5 billion in 2024](https://oxfordbusinessgroup.com/reports/oman/2025-report/ict/enhanced-connectivity-economic-diversification-drive-sees-efforts-to-expand-internet-access-boost-cybersecurity-and-integrate-new-technologies-overview/) and is projected to reach $9.1 billion by 2029, at a compound annual growth rate of 8.9%. There are now [6,828 registered ICT companies](https://investoman.om/start-investing/sectors/ict-36) operating in the Sultanate. Three mobile operators collectively run 5,893 5G sites, delivering 91% population coverage and median download speeds of 259.94 Mbps. Both Ooredoo and Vodafone Oman have deployed standalone 5G cores, enabling mission-critical applications across ports, oil fields, and remote industrial environments. [98% of Oman households had internet access](https://oxfordbusinessgroup.com/reports/oman/2025-report/ict/enhanced-connectivity-economic-diversification-drive-sees-efforts-to-expand-internet-access-boost-cybersecurity-and-integrate-new-technologies-overview/) as of September 2024. The country ranked 50th in the [Network Readiness Index 2024](https://oxfordbusinessgroup.com/reports/oman/2025-report/ict/enhanced-connectivity-economic-diversification-drive-sees-efforts-to-expand-internet-access-boost-cybersecurity-and-integrate-new-technologies-overview/), ahead of many economies with far larger technology industries.

Every number points in the same direction: significant capital investment, clear political commitment, and a national infrastructure programme that is ahead of most regional peers.

And yet, a persistent gap sits at the centre of this expanding market. Not a shortage of products. Not a shortage of companies willing to sell them. The gap is in engineering. It is in the local capability to design, integrate, and own complex technology deployments across the full operational lifecycle of the infrastructure being built.

This article is for any organisation that has experienced that gap, or is about to. It examines why the gap exists, what it costs in practice, and what a genuinely engineering-led model looks like as an alternative.





## Oman's ICT Market: Investment Without Engineering Depth

The scale of Oman's digital ambition is clear from the targets it has set. [Vision 2040](https://oman.om/en/home-top-level/eparticipation/oman-vision-2040) commits to growing the digital economy's share of GDP from approximately 2% in 2021 to 5% by 2030 and 10% by 2040. The National Programme for AI and Advanced Digital Technologies, launched in September 2024, sets out a framework for AI adoption across government, industrial, and commercial operations. Twelve new [data centres are planned](https://aiinoman.com/blog/oman-vision-2040-tech-businesses-meaning) across the Sultanate. An IoT-enabled smart electricity grid is in development. The government has committed to 80% of essential public services being delivered digitally.

This is infrastructure investment at a national scale, and it is creating real demand for technology services across energy, logistics, government, healthcare, manufacturing, and tourism.

The workforce being built to serve that demand tells a more complicated story. Analysis of the [Oman ICT market by Mordor Intelligence](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/oman-ict-market) found that Omantel Academy, launched in January 2026, committed to 1,200 advanced ICT training slots over four years, against a market that created 1,728 ICT positions in Q3 2024 alone: a supply deficit of more than threefold. Across the wider region, [Telecom Review has reported](https://www.telecomreview.com/articles/reports-and-coverage/29122-can-ai-close-the-ict-talent-gap/) a projected shortage of 4 million ICT jobs across the Middle East and Africa by 2030.

At the level of individual technology projects, the practical consequence of that shortage is that engineering decisions are often made by whoever is available to make them, rather than by specialists who understand the specific technical discipline a project requires. And in a market where many technology companies have not invested deeply in engineering capability in the first place, the gap becomes structural rather than simply a staffing issue.





## The Dominant Model: How Technology Delivery Actually Works in Oman

To understand the engineering gap, it helps to understand how technology delivery actually operates for many companies in this market.

A customer requirement surfaces. Often it arrives as a Request for Proposal with technical specifications already drafted. One or more companies that position themselves as system integrators review the requirement, identify a vendor whose product appears to match it, and engage that vendor's regional or local team. The vendor then performs the substantive technical work: solution architecture, radio frequency calculations, network design, bill of quantities, compliance documentation, and the engineering content of the RFP response.

The integrator incorporates this work into its own proposal, presents it under its letterhead, adds a commercial margin, and submits.

If the project is awarded, the vendor's regional engineering team typically leads implementation. Commissioning is completed. Handover documentation is transferred. The vendor's formal engagement ends. At no point in that process has the integrator performed the engineering. It has managed the procurement relationship, administered the project, and provided a commercial layer between the manufacturer and the customer.

That is a legitimate business function. But it is not systems integration in any engineering sense of the term.

[Help AG's assessment of the Middle East SI landscape](https://www.helpag.com/media-sources/systems-integrators-building-value/) identified one of the largest challenges across the region as the lack of technical expertise, noting that the growing competencies being demanded by customers are specifically in design and engineering of solutions and in the actual deployment. The challenge is structural: companies that have not invested in building genuine engineering teams cannot develop the design capability that would make them meaningfully different from a distributor.





## What Happens When the Vendor's Team Goes Home

The consequences of the reseller model become most visible after a deployment is complete.

New infrastructure is installed. The commissioning team finishes its engagement and departs. The customer holds the equipment, the documentation, and the nominal support relationship with the integrator. Then something changes. The network behaves unexpectedly under specific environmental conditions. A new device needs to be added to the system that was not part of the original scope. An expansion to a second site requires a design review that nobody involved in the original deployment is locally available to perform. Or a fault appears in a piece of equipment whose radio frequency behaviour was never fully understood by anyone based in Oman.

At that point, the customer discovers the precise boundary of the local capability the integrator possesses. In many cases, that boundary falls well short of what is required. The support ticket goes to the vendor. The vendor's support organisation, covering multiple markets and many simultaneous customers, works to its contracted response time. The fault that is directly affecting operations sits open, pending a response from a team in a different time zone.

The cost is not only financial, although unplanned downtime carries its own commercial consequence. The deeper cost is strategic. Knowledge about how the system works does not accumulate locally. Every service escalation resets to the manufacturer. The customer's digital infrastructure is dependent, in practical terms, on a company with no operational presence in Oman and no day-to-day accountability for how the system performs.

This is not a hypothetical risk. It is the standard experience for customers who have deployed technology through the dominant model and then encountered something outside the vendor's standard configuration.





## The Demand-Creation Vacuum

There is a second structural problem that is less visible but equally significant.

Most technology companies in Oman's market are reactive by design. They respond to requirements that exist in documented form, inside a procurement process. They optimise for winning RFPs, not for identifying what problems need to be solved before those RFPs are written.

Who creates the requirement in the first place? In most cases, the technology vendor does. The global manufacturer or specialist technology company approaches the end customer directly, identifies a problem, proposes a solution concept, and builds the technical case with the customer. That process creates the demand. Once the RFP is issued, multiple integrators enter the opportunity, contact the same manufacturer, and compete primarily on commercial terms.

The consequence is that technological innovation in Oman's market is largely imported. The ideas originate outside the country. The design originates outside the country. The engineering judgement that shapes what a customer actually deploys is exercised by people who will not be present when the system needs to be extended or repaired.

A company that genuinely engineers solutions does not wait for the RFP. It understands the customer's operations well enough to identify problems before the customer has articulated them as a procurement requirement. It introduces technologies the customer may not have encountered. It creates the opportunity rather than responding to it.





## What Technology-Agnostic Engineering Actually Means

The alternative to the reseller model starts from a different question.

Instead of asking "which of our vendor relationships produces a product that matches this requirement?", an engineering-led company asks: "what does this customer actually need to achieve, and what is the most effective technical architecture for that outcome?"

Technology selection follows from the engineering analysis, rather than preceding it.

[Being technology-agnostic](https://commercetools.com/blog/what-s-a-technology-agnostic-approach-and-why-is-it-a-core-trait-of-composable-commerce) means not being structurally tied to any specific manufacturer's product portfolio. It means the engineering team is free to identify the best-fit technology for each function within a solution, including technologies from specialist manufacturers that most of the market may not know. The value the customer receives is in the integration intelligence: the design work that makes multiple technologies from different manufacturers operate reliably as a single coherent system.

[Research on vendor-agnostic approaches](https://www.spreedly.com/blog/vendor-agnostic) consistently identifies the same outcomes: solutions that adapt more readily to changing requirements, lower long-term maintenance costs, and customers who retain genuine autonomy over their own infrastructure decisions rather than becoming dependent on a single supplier's product roadmap.

[Cornerstone Solutions](https://cornerstonesolutions.com.ng/blog/why-engineering-led-software-projects-outperform-vendor-driven-builds/) describes this distinction clearly: an engineering-led approach treats technology as a long-term business asset, with clear accountability and measurable outcomes. Technologies are selected because they solve specific problems. Complexity is introduced only when it is justified. The system is designed to evolve.

This requires a substantially different kind of organisation to execute: one that employs engineers who understand systems at the design level, not just at the procurement level.





## A Real Problem: When the Standard Answer Does Not Fit

Consider a specific scenario that illustrates the difference between the two models in practice.

A utility company in Oman wants to deploy smart meters across a distributed network of sites. The devices use [NB-IoT (Narrowband Internet of Things)](https://www.allaboutcircuits.com/industry-articles/narrowband-iot-power-efficient-connectivity-for-iot-and-smart-cities/) modules as their communication interface. NB-IoT is a well-established low-power wide-area network technology, built for exactly this class of application: low-bandwidth, low-power, geographically distributed IoT sensor deployment.

The problem is that several of the sites where these meters need to be installed are in locations where the required mobile network coverage simply does not exist. The NB-IoT module has no signal.

A company operating in reseller mode encounters a clear boundary. The product requires coverage. Coverage is absent at those sites. The scope is noted as out of reach. The customer is told to accept reduced coverage or to procure a separate, unintegrated solution independently. The vendor has no incentive to solve the problem differently because the standard solution has reached its documented limit.

An engineering-led approach arrives at a different question: what does the customer need to achieve across all sites, and is there an end-to-end architecture that achieves it?

The engineer examines the device interfaces, identifies what connectivity bridge could substitute for the missing mobile signal, and evaluates the options: a fixed wireless access link, a satellite backhaul segment, a private LTE micro-cell, or another specialist technology that can provide the bridging layer the application requires. The engineer then designs an architecture in which the bridging layer integrates transparently with the NB-IoT application, and the application cannot distinguish the bridged sites from native mobile connections.

The hardware in this solution may come from three different manufacturers. The value comes from none of them. It comes from the engineering layer that defines how they work together. That layer is what a genuine system integrator should be providing.





## What Oman Vision 2040 Actually Demands from Technology Companies

This distinction matters not only at the level of individual projects. It matters at the level of national infrastructure delivery.

[Vision 2040](https://oman.om/en/home-top-level/eparticipation/oman-vision-2040) has set precise targets for the digital economy. The programme is not an aspiration: it is a documented national commitment backed by investment in data centres, smart grid infrastructure, AI capability, and nationwide 5G coverage.

[Ericsson and the TRA's Oman Enterprise Summit in early 2026](https://www.ericsson.com/en/press-releases/5/2026/ericsson-and-telecommunications-regulatory-authority-host-oman-enterprise-summit-for-advancing-vision-2040) highlighted the specific connectivity requirements of the Sultanate's key industrial sectors: smart ports, digital oil and gas operations, advanced manufacturing, autonomous industrial processes, and mission-critical environments. These are not straightforward deployments. They involve complex integration of multiple systems, demanding reliability standards, and operational environments that standard equipment configurations were not designed for.

Achieving Vision 2040's outcomes in these sectors requires technology that works at the point of deployment, in the physical environments Oman actually presents: remote sites, high-temperature outdoor installations, radio frequency environments shaped by terrain and competing infrastructure, and locations where standard coverage assumptions simply do not apply.

It requires post-deployment support that is genuinely local. It requires engineering capability that can adapt a system when requirements change, integrate a new technology when a better option becomes available, and diagnose a fault with direct knowledge of the specific installation.

[Oxford Business Group's 2025 assessment of Oman's ICT sector](https://oxfordbusinessgroup.com/reports/oman/2025-report/ict/enhanced-connectivity-economic-diversification-drive-sees-efforts-to-expand-internet-access-boost-cybersecurity-and-integrate-new-technologies-overview/) noted that emerging technologies like IoT and AI are creating new opportunities alongside genuine challenges in scalability and adoption. Scalability and adoption, in practice, usually come down to the same thing: whether the organisation deploying the technology has the engineering knowledge to make it work beyond the initial installation.

The reseller model is not structurally capable of delivering those outcomes at scale. It was not designed to. It was designed to win contracts.





## Eight Capabilities That Separate Engineering-Led Firms from Resellers

There are eight specific capabilities that define the difference between a genuine engineering-led technology company and a conventional reseller operating as an integrator.

**1. Demand Creation**

A real technology partner identifies customer problems before an RFP exists. It understands the customer's operational environment well enough to introduce solutions the customer may not have known were available. It creates the opportunity rather than waiting to respond to one.

**2. Solution Architecture**

The engineering team produces the technical design internally. The architecture is the output of an independent analysis of the customer's requirements, constraints, and options. It is not a vendor's pre-packaged proposal repurposed for the customer's document format.

**3. Technology Scouting**

The best technology for a specific problem may come from a specialist manufacturer that is unknown to the wider market. An engineering-led company evaluates technologies globally, selecting for fit rather than for the convenience of an existing partnership.

**4. Systems Integration**

When a solution requires multiple technologies from different manufacturers to operate together, the integration itself is the product. The engineering team designs, implements, and tests the interfaces. The customer receives a coherent, working system, not a collection of independently procured components.

**5. RF and Network Engineering**

Wireless deployments require real engineering: frequency planning, coverage simulation, interference analysis, antenna selection, and link budget calculations. These are technical disciplines performed by specialists. Attaching a manufacturer's coverage estimate to a proposal is a different activity.

**6. Implementation and Commissioning**

Taking responsibility for a deployment means staying present through commissioning, verifying that the installed system performs as designed, and not closing the project until it does. It is not the same as delivering equipment to a site.

**7. Local Technical Support**

Level 1 and Level 2 support should be provided by engineers who are physically based in Oman and who understand the specific configuration of the system they are supporting. Support that escalates every problem to the manufacturer's international team is not local support in any meaningful sense.

**8. Lifecycle Ownership**

A deployment is not complete when the purchase order is fulfilled. An engineering-led company stays engaged across the operational life of the solution: firmware updates, hardware refresh cycles, performance optimisation, and integration of new capabilities as requirements change.

## How to Evaluate a Technology Partner in Oman

If you are procuring technology infrastructure in Oman, there are practical questions that quickly separate engineering-led organisations from resellers.

**Ask who produced the technical design.** If the answer is "we worked with our vendor partner to develop the proposal," the design came from the manufacturer. The integrator is a commercial intermediary, not an engineering company.

**Ask where the engineering team is located.** If post-deployment problems are escalated to a vendor hub in another country, local technical support is not a service being offered in any substantive sense.

**Ask how the technology was selected over alternatives.** A genuinely technology-agnostic provider should be able to describe the evaluation process and explain why the proposed technology was chosen over other options that were assessed. If the answer defaults to an existing vendor partnership, the evaluation may not have been independent.

**Ask for an example where the standard vendor solution did not work, and what they did about it.** The response to this question almost always makes clear exactly where real engineering capability resides within the organisation.

Oman's physical environment presents installation conditions that global vendor documentation does not always address. They affect whether a system performs as specified or whether it requires repeated site visits to correct problems that were never accounted for in the original design.

Procurement expectations in Omani government and semi-government markets, the documentation requirements of major industrial customers, and the commercial relationships built over time in this specific market are all factors that shape whether a project moves efficiently or stalls at each contract milestone.

Engineering decisions made without that accumulated local knowledge produce systems that perform differently in the field than they did in the specification document. ## The Market Oman's Digital Ambition Deserves

Oman has the investment, the infrastructure, and a clearly articulated national vision to build a sophisticated digital economy. The 5G network is built. The data centre capacity is expanding. The policy framework is aligned with a credible long-term plan.

What the market still needs, in sufficient quantity, are organisations that bring genuine engineering depth to the projects being funded by that investment.

Companies that start from the customer's problem, not a product portfolio. That design independently rather than repackaging a vendor's documentation. That take accountability for outcomes across the operational life of the system, not just at the point of delivery. That carry local knowledge which compounds across engagements, making each subsequent project more effective than the last.

That model of technology delivery does not have to be expensive or available only to the largest government programmes. Industrial operators, private sector organisations, and mid-market customers across the Sultanate face the same infrastructure challenges as the largest enterprises. They deserve the same standard of engineering engagement.

Oman's technology sector is at a point where investment has clearly outpaced engineering capability. Closing that gap is not a government programme. It is a commercial decision that technology companies operating in the Sultanate need to make: to build the engineering depth the market requires, rather than continuing to function as managed intermediaries for the manufacturers they represent.
